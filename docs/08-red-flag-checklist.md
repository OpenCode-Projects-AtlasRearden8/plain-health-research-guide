# The Red-Flag Checklist

*Twelve flags to run any health headline, post, pitch, or study against. Range: 0 flags = healthy skepticism, take the story gently; 5+ flags = close the tab with affection.*

> Keep this page open next to any story you're checking — or print it. Each flag is a single yes/no question with a one-line reminder of *why*.

## The flags

### Flag 1 — Denominator missing

**Test:** Does the story give a percent or "n-fold" increase with no "out of how many"?

**Reminder:** Relative risk without a baseline is volume without a speaker. ("Risk rose 50%," …from 2 in 10,000 to 3 in 10,000.)

### Flag 2 — Correlation in causal clothing

**Test:** Does "linked to," "associated with," or "tied to" appear near a verb like *causes, prevents, protects, gives you*?

**Reminder:** They measured weather and umbrella sales; the story reported umbrellas cause rain.

### Flag 3 — The species teleporter

**Test:** Is the study in mice, rats, cells, or a dish — while the framing talks about *your* breakfast?

**Reminder:** Animal work is legitimate science. Teleporting it to humans isn't.

### Flag 4 — One-study eruption

**Test:** Does the story rest entirely on one new study, with no mention of what came before?

**Reminder:** The newest paper is one word in a long conversation, not the conversation itself.

### Flag 5 — The decorative expert

**Test:** Is the quoted expert's *field* different from the study's topic — or vaguely described ("a wellness expert says")?

**Reminder:** Title check beats credential check. What's their actual research in, and do they gain anything?

### Flag 6 — Question-mark headline

**Test:** Does the headline end in a question mark?

**Reminder:** Betteridge's law: if a headline is a question, the accurate answer is usually "no" — the mark exists to keep the claim deniable.

### Flag 7 — Preprint passing as settled

**Test:** Does the story cite research that hasn't been peer reviewed, without saying so?

**Reminder:** Preprints are normal and useful. Presenting them as approved findings is not.

### Flag 8 — Anecdote standing in for data

**Test:** Does the story lean on a personal story ("I was bedridden, now I hike") *as the evidence*?

**Reminder:** Stories are real experiences and unreliable witnesses in one body. Sample size: one.

### Flag 9 — Miracle grammar

**Test:** Does the pitch use "cures," "reverses," "detoxes," "doctors don't want you to know," or one product for many diseases?

**Reminder:** Real breakthroughs are narrow, published, loudly discussed, and adopted by clinics — they don't require a conspiracy to be kept from you.

### Flag 10 — The bouncing numbers

**Test:** If there's a p-value or CI, does the CI cross 1.0 (OR/RR) — or is the "significant" effect under ~10% relative with a tiny absolute difference?

**Reminder:** Statistically detectable ≠ practically meaningful. Check both dials.

### Flag 11 — The missing comparison

**Test:** Can the story not state what treatment was *compared to* — placebo, standard care, or nothing?

**Reminder:** "Better" is only a word until it says better *than what*.

### Flag 12 — The urgency meter

**Test:** Does the piece push urgency — "act now," "before it's banned," countdown timers, "share before it's deleted"?

**Reminder:** Actual clinical science doesn't Black-Friday you. Urgency is a price-setting device.

## Scoring, honestly

- **0–2 flags** — Probably an imperfect port of a real paper. Still check the numbers; still be careful with your own health decisions.
- **3–4 flags** — Distortion is present. Consider reading the abstract yourself or running it through the [worksheet](07-study-reading-worksheet.html).
- **5+ flags** — You're not reading a summary of research; you're reading marketing with a lab coat on.

One small grace note: the score is about the *piece you're reading*, not about you — spotting six flags is the skill working, not a failure. And nothing on this page substitutes for your own care team; if a story here raises a question about your real health, take the *question* to a clinician.
