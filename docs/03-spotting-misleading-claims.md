# Spotting Misleading Health Claims

*A field guide to the tricks that turn modest studies into bold headlines.*

## First, a calibrating thought

Most bad health journalism isn't a lie. It's a **funnel**. Each stage — study abstract → press release → newsroom → headline → social media caption — has incentives that reward sharpness and punishes nuance. By the time it reaches you, "we found a weak association in mice" has become "DOCTORS HATE THIS FOOD."

Your job isn't to catch singular villains. It's to recognize the *shapes* of distortion. Here are the ones you'll meet repeatedly.

## Shape 1: The disappearing denominator

**The trick:** A percent increase with no count attached.

**Example shape:** "Eating daily X raises disease risk by 40%."

**The repair question:** *Out of how many?* If baseline risk is 1 in 10,000 and it goes to 1.4 in 10,000, "40% up" is technically accurate and practically yawn-worthy. (Full treatment in [Numbers in Plain English](02-numbers-in-plain-english.html).)

## Shape 2: Correlation dressed as causation

**The trick:** "Linked to," "associated with," "tied to" — then a causative verb sneaks in by the headline's end.

**Example shape:** "People who drink green tea have 20% fewer strokes — because green tea shields your vessels."

**The repair questions:**

- Could healthy-user bias explain it? (People who drink green tea may also exercise, sleep, and smoke less.)
- What is the direction of the association — or did they even check? (Sometimes it's U-shaped, J-shaped, or driven by sick people changing habits.)
- Was this a randomized trial? No? Then hold the causal verbs like a hot pan.

## Shape 3: The mice-to-humans teleporter

**The trick:** A result in rodents, cells, or a test tube is reported with humans as the implied beneficiary.

**Example shape:** "New study finds common sweetener causes memory loss." *(…in 12 mice, at doses no human eats.)*

**The repair question:** *Was the study in people?* Animal work is a legitimate scientific step; media routinely skip it to the "applies to your breakfast" stage. A good rule: if the word "mice" appears anywhere in the actual paper and nowhere in the headline, be suspicious.

## Shape 4: The one-study eruption

**The trick:** "A NEW STUDY SAYS" — as though every new study arrives at truth fully formed, like Athena from Zeus's head.

**The reality:** Findings travel a life cycle. Early small studies show effects; larger, better studies often shrink or widen them. There's even a name for the pattern of early exciting claims being tamed by later replication: the **Proteus phenomenon**.

**The repair question:** *What does the weight of evidence say — not just the latest paper?* A systematic review or meta-analysis carries more weight than the newest preprint.

## Shape 5: The expert costume

**The trick:** Citing an expert whose expertise is decorative.

**The repair questions:**

- Is this person a researcher in *this exact field*? ("Cardiac surgeon" ≠ cancer epidemiologist ≠ nutrition researcher.)
- Do they have an interest in the outcome? (Foundation-funded, company-funded, or selling a book called *The Green Tea Stroke Cure*?)
- Did they author the study, or are they opining at a distance? *(Opining is fine! Just label it.)*

## Shape 6: The clickbait question headline

**The trick:** "Could X Prevent Cancer?" — a question mark that lets the story plant a claim while keeping a technical exit. This is so common it has a name: **Betteridge's law** — any headline ending in a question mark can be answered "no."

**The repair question:** *What does the actual paper conclude?* Not the headline. Frequently the answer to a question-headline is, elegantly, "no."

## Shape 7: The miracle cure's cousin — the "surprisingly simple" trick

**The trick:** Health advice packaged as forbidden knowledge your doctor doesn't want you to know.

**The honest reality, delivered with warmth:** there are no miracle treatments hidden from medicine and discoverable by podcasts. Real breakthroughs are loudly published in indexed journals and adopted by clinics, and — much less excitingly — they're usually things you've already heard: sleep, movement, not smoking, moderation.

**The repair question:** *If this treatment really worked, who would have an incentive to keep it quiet and why would they succeed for >5 years?*

## Shape 8: The preprint that skipped peer review

**The trick:** Exciting result posted to a preprint server (researchers' open-driveway), reported as settled science in under 48 hours.

**Important nuance, in fairness:** preprints are a normal, often-good part of modern science — especially in fast-moving situations. They are just *not yet* checked. Treat them like a draft you read over a friend's shoulder: informative, provisional, not final.

**The repair question:** *Has this been peer reviewed, and who reviewed it?*

## Shape 9: The numerator game (survivorship & selection)

**The trick:** "My grandmother smoked and lived to 99!" — an absolute fact, weighted as if it were equal to the population-level data, which it is not.

**The repair reframing:** population statistics tell you what's likely; anecdotes tell you what's possible. You are planning around the likely — that's not negativity, that's just how probability works when it's kind to you.

## All shapes at a glance

| Shape | Signature phrase | Repair question |
|---|---|---|
| Disappearing denominator | "40% higher risk" | Out of how many? |
| Correlation as causation | "Linked to" | What could confound this? |
| Mice teleporter | "Causes memory loss" | In humans or mice? |
| One-study eruption | "A new study says" | What does the full evidence say? |
| Expert costume | "Experts warn" | Who is this expert an expert *in*? |
| Question headline | "Could X prevent Y?" | What does the paper say? |
| Miracle cure | "Doctors won't tell you" | Who benefits from the secrecy? |
| Preprint panic | "New preprint finds" | Has it been peer reviewed? |
| Numerator game | "My aunt smoked and…" | Possible ≠ likely — what's the base rate? |

## How the repair typically goes — a full worked example

**Headline:** *"Coffee figures stir new fears: study links morning coffee to heart rhythm problems."*

**Abstract version:** a cohort study of ~300,000 people found that heavy coffee intake was *associated* with a modestly increased rate of one specific arrhythmia, while light-to-moderate intake showed no increase; the authors note multiple confounders and recommend cautious interpretation.

**What the funnel did:** kept the "risk" and the "coffee," dropped "heavy," "one arrhythmia," and "modest," added "new fears."

**Your repair, in 90 seconds:**

1. Who was studied, and what was "heavy"? (The paper says: mostly middle-aged adults, mean age ~50.)
2. How big is the effect in absolute terms? (A modest relative increase on a low baseline.)
3. What design? (Cohort — so no cause.)
4. Cross-check the [Red-Flag Checklist](08-red-flag-checklist.html): the headline trips flags 2, 3, and 5 before you even finish your coffee.

Which is what the checklist is for: not cynicism — calibration.
