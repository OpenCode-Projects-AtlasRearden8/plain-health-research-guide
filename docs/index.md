# Reading Health Research Without a PhD

*A plain-English manual for headlines, abstracts, and study numbers — written like a friend would.*

## Who this is for

You saw a headline this morning. It said coffee is bad for you. Or maybe it said coffee is *good* for you. Tuesday's headline said the opposite, and both quoted "a new study."

You're not a scientist. You don't have time to become one. But you'd like to know: **is any of this worth changing your life over?**

If that's you, this manual is for you. It's for:

- Anyone who reads health news and feels whiplash from contradictory headlines
- Patients and caregivers who want to understand a paper their doctor mentioned
- People who want to fact-check a viral health claim *before* sharing it
- Anyone who has ever felt talked down to by a wall of jargon in a "Plain Language Summary"

You do not need a science background. You need curiosity, a little skepticism, and about an hour.

## What this manual will and won't do

**What it will do:**

- Decode an abstract (that summary block at the top of a research paper) section by section
- Translate the scary-sounding numbers — odds ratios, p-values, percentiles — into plain words
- Teach you to spot the tricks that turn a mediocre study into a confident-sounding headline
- Show you how to use AI to translate jargon, and *then* verify that the AI didn't get it wrong
- Give you printable helpers: a study-reading worksheet, a red-flag checklist, and a glossary

**What it will never do:**

- Tell you what to do about your own health. This manual is **education only — never medical advice**. If something here raises a question about your own care, that question belongs with your doctor, not with this guide.

## The three sentences worth remembering

If you read nothing else, hold on to these:

1. **One study is a data point, not a verdict.** Science builds claims over many studies; headlines single out one.
2. **"Associated with" does not mean "caused by."** People who drink red wine and live long lives also tend to do a lot of other things differently.
3. **Size and quality of the study beat the punchiness of the headline.** A careful study of 10,000 people beats a dramatic study of 20 — usually toward boring, sensible results.
