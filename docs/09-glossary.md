# Glossary

*Plain-English definitions of the words health research actually uses. Roughly alphabetical, deliberately casual, and occasionally opinionated where the field itself argues.*

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
|---|---|
| **Absolute risk** | Your actual chance of an outcome, counted plainly: "2 in 1,000 people." The number that least wants to be in a headline, and the one most worth finding. |
| **Abstract** | A compressed summary of a whole study — introduction, methods, results, conclusion, in a few paragraphs. Useful, lossy, and the only part most people ever read. |
| **Adverse event** | Anything bad that happens during a study — whether or not it was caused by the treatment. Sprinkled carefully, it says a lot about a treatment's real-world personality. |
| **Bias (in studies)** | A systematic tilt — in who's included, what's measured, or whose memory is used — rather than simple sloppiness. Everyone's studies lean somewhere; good authors spend a lot of length on theirs. |
| **Blinded (single/double)** | People (or people *and* their doctors) not knowing who got the real treatment vs. the placebo — because beliefs contaminate measurements. Double-blind is the more thorough flavor. |
| **Case-control study** | A backward-looking study: compare people with an outcome against people without it, looking for what differed. Fast and cheap; leans heavily on memory, which leans badly. |
| **Case report / case series** | Descriptions of what happened to a patient (or a handful of them). Valuable for spotting *that something exists*; useless for saying *how often it happens*. |
| **Cohort study** | Follows a group over years and compares outcomes between people who happen to differ (coffee drinkers vs. not). The source of most "X is linked to Y" news. Good for relationships, soft for causation. |
| **Confidence interval (CI)** | The wiggle room around a result: "best guess 0.67, data consistent with 0.50–0.83." If the interval crosses 1.0 for a ratio, the data can't rule out "no effect." |
| **Confounding** | A hidden third factor that genuinely causes the apparent link. (Ice cream sales are associated with drownings because *summer causes both*.) The uninvited guest at every observational study. |
| **Correlation** | Two things moving together. Necessary for a causal story; hilariously insufficient, as seen in the ice cream. |
| **Crossover trial** | Each participant tries both the treatment and the comparison, in separate phases. Efficient; washout periods (letting the first treatment clear) are the fussy part. |
| **Denominator** | What a percent is a percent *of*. Missing denominators are where most scary numbers go to hide. |
| **Effect size** | How big the difference actually is, in units a human can feel. The answer to "so... how much?" after a study announces it found *something*. |
| **Endpoint (primary/secondary)** | The outcome a study officially commits to measuring. Findings on secondary or surprise endpoints deserve a squint — that's how "we checked 90 things and one looked good" gets dressed up. |
| **Exclusion criteria** | Who the study deliberately kept *out* (people with other conditions, kids, pregnant people, and so on). Defines how far the findings can honestly travel. |
| **RCT (randomized controlled trial)** | Participants randomly assigned to treatment vs. not-treatment. The cleanest single-study design for asking *whether* something causes something. |
| **Hazard ratio (HR)** | Like a risk ratio, but for how fast people reach an outcome over follow-up time. Same reading rule as always: ≈1 means nothing much. |
| **Meta-analysis** | A formal pooling of many studies into one combined estimate. Powerful when the included studies are good; "garbage in, garbage out" still applies with extra steps. |
| **Non-significant** | "Too indistinct from chance to claim a finding." *Not* the same as "proven absent" — often it means the study was too small to reliably detect an effect. |
| **Null hypothesis** | The boring default ("the treatment does nothing") that a p-value measures surprise against. |
| **Odds ratio (OR)** | Compares the *odds* of an outcome between groups. ≈1 means no difference; 0.5 or 2.0 means a real gap worth attention; still correlation-shaped, not automatic causation. |
| **p-value** | Roughly: if nothing were really happening, how surprising would data this extreme be? A threshold, not a certification of truth or size. |
| **Peer review** | Other researchers checking a paper before publication. Necessary, imperfect, not a certification of truth — it's a quality screen, not a quality guarantee. |
| **Placebo** | A sham treatment, so the comparison isolates the actual treatment effect. Placebo responses in pain, nausea, and mood are famously large and famously real. |
| **Placebo-controlled** | Versus a placebo rather than versus nothing, so expectations don't leak into the result. |
| **Preprint** | A paper posted before peer review. Normal, often good, and explicitly "not yet checked" — treat it like a draft, not a verdict. |
| **Power** | A study's ability to detect an effect that's actually there. Under-powered studies hand out too-small doses of certainty both ways: they can miss real effects *and* exaggerate ones they do find. |
| **Publication bias** | Studies with exciting results are written up and published more readily than null ones — so the visible literature skews sunny. |
| **Relative risk** | The *ratio* between two groups' risks. Same data as absolute risk, ten times the emotional volume. Always paired with its baseline friend. |
| **Retraction** | A paper being formally withdrawn (errors, misconduct). Rare, tracked, worth checking — a paper that's been retracted should never, ever resurface in your news feed uncited. |
| **Sample size (n)** | How many people (or animals, or cells) were involved. Shapes how much a finding can literally carry. |
| **Secondary outcome** | Any outcome a study measured that wasn't its official question. More interpretive caution advised than the primary. |
| **Selection bias** | When the people studied differ from the people the findings will be about (a fit young group; a hospital-only group). Limits travels. |
| **Statistical significance** | "Probably not a fluke at a chosen threshold (often p < 0.05)." About flukes; not about importance. Famous for being mistaken for its better-looking twin, *clinical significance*, which asks whether patients should care. |
| **Surrogate endpoint** | Standing-in measure (cholesterol level, biomarker, tumor-shrinkage-on-scan) instead of the real thing (heart attacks, survival). Extremely useful, sometimes optimistic. |
| **Systematic review** | A structured, reproducible sweep across everything published on a question. The best single sentence in the abstract, when present. |
| | |

*Definitions here are educational simplifications, not formal definitions — the sort of thing you'd write down to remember them, not the sort you'd cite. And of course: education only, never medical advice.*
