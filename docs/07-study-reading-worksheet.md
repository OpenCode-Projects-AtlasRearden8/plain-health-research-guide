## Section 5 — Red flags (carried over from [the checklist](08-red-flag-checklist.html))

14. Which flags did the story trip?

    - [ ] Denominator missing / relative-risk theater
    - [ ] Correlation word used causally
    - [ ] Mice/cells implied to be humans
    - [ ] One-study eruption ("a new study says")
    - [ ] Vague or decorative expert
    - [ ] Question-mark headline
    - [ ] Preprint treated as settled
    - [ ] Anecdote weighted like data
    - [ ] Miracle-cure language ("cures," "doctors won't tell you," "detox")

## Section 6 — The verdict, in three honest boxes

15. **Strength of evidence** (from this paper alone):

    > [ ] weak — anecdote-level   [ ] suggestive   [ ] reasonably strong   [ ] strong

16. **Would this apply to people like me?** *(Who, age-wise and health-wise, was actually studied?)*

    > _______________________________________________

17. **What should I do with this?** Pick at most one:

    - [ ] Share it (only with the accurate framing, not the headline)
    - [ ] Add it to a list of questions for my care team ([chapter 5](05-questions-for-your-care-team.html))
    - [ ] Just keep an eye on future research — no action now
    - [ ] Nothing — quietly discard it and move on with my day

## The friendly closer

If your worksheet came out with mostly "suggestive" and "no action now" — congratulations, that's what an accurate reading usually looks like. Boring verdicts aren't failed readings; they're successful ones. Only the *rare* worksheet earns a phone call to your care team, and that's genuinely the system working.

Education only — never medical advice. If a worksheet raises something that touches your own health, that question's next stop is a clinician, not a comment section.
